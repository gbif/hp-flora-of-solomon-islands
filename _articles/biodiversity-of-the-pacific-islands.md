  The many islands of the Pacific and some of the surrounding areas have long been biodiversity hotspots. Besides being rich in endemic species, the region has also begun to suffer extensive human-caused destruction. This article first discusses the term "biodiversity" and its definition, and the importance the international community now places on biodiversity. It then uses the five years of field surveys and collecting carried out in the Solomon Islands under the "Solomon Islands Resource Plant Survey and Flora Compilation Project," led primarily by the National Museum of Natural Science, as an example to introduce biodiversity conservation work.

The term "biodiversity" was coined only in 1986, near the end of the last century. It originally referred to surveying and recording all the animal, plant, fungal, and microbial species on Earth and compiling them into checklists. As many scholars came to recognize the close and complex interdependence among living organisms, both similar and dissimilar, the concept of biodiversity was expanded to cover the variability of all organisms in every ecosystem. It now includes not only life at the levels of individuals, populations, communities, geographic distributions, and ecosystems, but also at the molecular level, including genes. If we bring "humans" into the discussion, biodiversity simply means that people and all other living things share the biosphere of Earth, thriving together and carrying life on from generation to generation. In fact, biodiversity has ecological and economic, scientific and educational, and cultural and ethical value, and how to strike a balance between development and conservation has long been a subject of coordination, cooperation, discussion, and research among nations.

To draw global attention to this major issue and address it, the United Nations convened the "Earth Summit" (also known as the United Nations Conference on Environment and Development) in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, in June 1992. Since then, more than 190 countries have signed the Convention on Biological Diversity, whose first objective is the conservation of biological diversity, and international work on the conservation and sustainable use of biodiversity has been actively pursued. The first task was to choose which places or regions should be targeted for immediate biodiversity conservation. Building on the idea of "hotspots" that Norman Myers put forward in The Environmentalist in 1988 and 1990, Myers and other scholars revised and analyzed those articles in 2000 to compile a list of hotspots and give the term a clearer definition: the biogeographic regions on Earth that are richest in life and most endangered. They also set two strict criteria for designating a hotspot. The first concerns the number of endemic species: the region must contain at least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species, or at least 0.5% of the world's total. The second concerns the degree of habitat threat: the region must have already lost at least 70% of its original habitat. Under these criteria, 36 places have so far been designated as global biodiversity hotspots. In other words, these 36 hotspots are the geographic regions that currently hold Earth's richest and most threatened reservoirs of biological resources, and they have been recognized as the most important ecosystems in the world. These hotspots are home to nearly 60% of the world's plants, amphibians and reptiles, birds, and mammals, with very high proportions of endemic species. They therefore preserve the greatest share of biodiversity for its continued existence, safeguarding not only individual organisms but also information at the molecular level. Biodiversity conservation is one of the major global issues of the 21st century and the foundation of sustainable human development. Studies indicate that global biodiversity is being lost rapidly, and the most striking figure is that species are going extinct at 100 to 1,000 times the background rate! With populations rising year after year, natural resources are being consumed ever faster. Add to this all kinds of environmental pollution, global warming, and the spread of invasive species, and the biodiversity crisis has become a reality that humanity must face today.

The hotspots for Pacific biodiversity discussed in this article are mainly grouped in the "Southeast Asia and Asia-Pacific" region. They include three hotspots: the East Melanesian Islands (from the Melanesian islands to northeastern Australia), New Caledonia (the New Caledonian archipelago in the southwestern Pacific), and Polynesia–Micronesia (the islands of the South Pacific). Besides lying in the tropics, they consist of countless islands scattered like stars. This article focuses on the Solomon Islands, where we carried out the "Resource Plant Survey and Flora Compilation Project," and broadly speaking, the biodiversity of these hotspots can be used for mutual reference and comparison. If we also include the other six hotspots in this region—New Zealand, the Philippines, Southwest Australia, the Forests of East Australia, Sundaland, and Wallacea—as well as Japan in the "East Asia" region, nearly the entire western half of the Pacific is covered. However, if we narrow the scope to the Solomon Islands, which I want to discuss, we need only consider the three hotspots mentioned above.

Located in the South Pacific, Solomon Islands maintained official diplomatic relations with the Republic of China (Taiwan) from 1983 to 2019, and the two countries had embassies in each other's capitals. The country lies about 1,800 km northeast of Australia, between 155° and 168° E and 5° and 12° S, and consists of nearly a thousand islands east of Papua New Guinea. It is divided into nine provinces: Central Province, Choiseul Province, Guadalcanal Province, Isabel Province, Makira-Ulawa (or San Christobal) Province, Malaita Province, Rennell and Bellona (Renbel-Bellona) Province, Temotu Province, and Western Province. The capital, Honiara, is in Guadalcanal Province. Most of the country's nearly thousand islands lie below 1,000 m in elevation, although the highest peak, Mount Popomanaseu (2,484 m), stretches across southern Guadalcanal. Solomon Islands lies in an earthquake zone and has a land area of about 28,400 km², more than 90% of which is covered by tropical rainforest. It is rich in forest products, is home to many endemic vertebrates, and is an important fishing ground in the South Pacific. Deposits of gold, nickel, copper, cobalt, bauxite, and other minerals are found at many sites, and the national population is about 500,000.

At the end of 2011, after discussions with Dr. Tetsuo KOYAMA, then director of the Kochi Prefectural Makino Botanical Garden in Japan (hereafter MBG), our National Museum of Natural Science (hereafter NMNS) was asked to take over the "Solomon Islands Resource Plant Survey and Flora Compilation Project," which MBG had been carrying out in collaboration with the Solomon Islands Ministry of Forestry and Research (hereafter MOFR). Dr. Koyama estimated that Taiwan has a little over 4,000 native species, whereas the Solomon Islands lie in the tropics and span nearly 13 degrees of longitude and 7 degrees of latitude (the main island of Taiwan spans about 2 degrees of longitude and 3.5 degrees of latitude), with dozens of peaks higher than 1,500 m. He therefore estimated that the Solomon Islands have roughly 7,000 native plant species. Under the leadership of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, the Ministry of Education, the International Cooperation and Development Fund (ICDF), and NMNS, Mr. Dickson MUA, Minister of the Solomon Islands MOFR, and Dr. Tetsuo Koyama, Director of MBG in Japan, were invited to Taiwan. On June 5, 2012, Minister Mua, Director Koyama, and NMNS Director Dr. Wei-Hsin SUN held a tripartite signing ceremony in Taipei, and from July 1 of that year Taiwan took over the resource plant survey and flora compilation project. At the same time, NMNS invited the Dr. Cecilia Koo Botanic Conservation Center (KBCC), the Taiwan Forestry Research Institute (TFRI) of the Council of Agriculture, the Endemic Species Research Institute (ESRI) of the Council of Agriculture, and related departments of domestic universities to jointly participate in this large-scale international plant diversity and conservation project. Because the Solomon Islands comprise so many islands and the initial phase of the project was only five years, we decided after discussion to organize the work by the country's nine provinces, hoping that our team members would set foot in every province within five years. We would further go to local logging areas to collect, including dried plant specimens for the collection, pollen/spore and anatomical materials, dried DNA materials, fruits and seeds, live seedlings, and local ethnobotanical plants (e.g., those used for food and daily necessities), as well as species with potential economic value (e.g., for medicine, horticulture, and crops). I traveled to the Solomon Islands with Dr. Chia-Wei LI, CEO of KBCC, for reconnaissance and discussions, and he generously decided to build a net house at the headquarters of the Taiwan Technical Mission in the capital and donate it to the Solomon Islands government. Thus the workflow for this plant diversity survey and conservation effort gradually took shape: researchers collected in the field in the Solomon Islands, preparing dried specimens and other research materials, and brought fruits, seeds, and seedlings back to the capital to be grown in the Technical Mission's net house. Once the plants were established, they were shipped back to Taiwan together with the dried plant specimens (dried specimens were mailed to NMNS, while live materials went directly to KBCC). A duplicate set of dried plant specimens was kept at the Solomon Islands National Herbarium, and live plants were handed over to local institutions for display, education, and outreach. The Makino Botanical Garden asked only that a set of Cyperaceae specimens be sent to them.

Although the Solomon Islands were affected to some extent during World War II, their islands are scattered over a wide area, so the forests have remained in fairly good condition. However, logging companies operate in the country, and logging is one of the government's important sources of revenue, so a complete logging ban may still be some time away. At the start of the project, we were fortunate to meet a Taiwanese businessman, Mr. Chia-Hung WEI, who introduced us to several owners of logging operations in the Solomon Islands. They agreed to let us survey and collect in areas they had already logged or were currently logging. Consider that a large tree may stand 30 m tall, with a different microclimate at each height, so the species that grow at a given height naturally differ from those at 5 or 10 m above the ground. When collecting plants, it is already hard enough to see what is growing epiphytically on branches within 15 m, let alone species higher up. Logging companies want only the trunks and discard all the branches, so when a tree is felled, we can go in first to search for and collect the epiphytes on those branches. Under the overarching banner of economic development, our ability to "stop" deforestation is minimal. But by taking advantage of the timing and location, we can record and preserve these species that grow high in the canopy, learning about more species while also conserving as many of them as possible.

Outside the capital, electricity in the Solomon Islands is unreliable in many places, even in provincial capitals, let alone the more remote rural areas we visited. Apart from solar power, drying specimens usually had to rely on gas burners. Gas had to be carried from the provincial capital to each rural area, so how long we could stay in the countryside depended on how many gas cylinders we could bring! This is why a week in one place was about the maximum; beyond that, specimens could not be dried and would mold and rot, lowering their quality or even rendering them unusable. Since we usually asked the central MOFR to send staff to accompany us on field trips, when we arrived in each provincial capital we would usually meet with the local forestry office to inform them of the project, even presenting a certificate from the central MOFR. We would then go up into the mountains to collect accompanied by locally assigned staff, or find local forest landowners to take us to collect on their land. In these cases, branches higher than 20 m were usually out of reach! There were exceptions, however. In April 2015, we went collecting in northern Malaita Province. On the day before we left, an elderly landowner showed us around his land. In an open area we saw several plants resembling screw pines, some tall and some short. Seeing me check these plants for flowers or fruits (most had neither), the old landowner asked our guide what I was looking for. After the translation, he pointed to a large tree about 20 m tall and said that one had flowers and fruits (translated by the guide, of course)! Looking at such a tall tree, with flowers at the very top (which I actually could not see), and knowing none of my tools could reach them, I could only smile and tell him that the population here seemed fairly large, so maybe I could collect it next time. The old man replied: there are a fair number of these plants, but that one is flowering—do you want it? I had to tell the truth: of course I want it, but I can't reach it... The old man was delighted to hear this. He first handed his machete to his grandson, who chopped for about a minute without making much progress. The old man immediately went over and took the machete back, and in less than a minute the tree came down (after witnessing the old man's prowess, I never again dared to point high up a tree and say I wanted that specimen)! The inflorescence was about 2 m long, and we made all of it into specimens. We could not take the trunk with us, so we could only add some leaves to the specimens. Later, when we returned to the capital and visited the botanical garden, Curator Sirikolo immediately exclaimed upon seeing the specimen: *Sararanga sinuosa*! This species belongs to the genus *Sararanga* of the Pandanaceae, which has only two species worldwide, distributed in the Philippines and the Solomon Islands!

Of course, we could never expect to cover every kind and quantity of species in these five years, and the species to be conserved had to be selected first, for example the Orchidaceae, Zingiberaceae, Araceae, Arecaceae, ferns, Begoniaceae, Melastomataceae, Lecythidaceae, and epiphytic Rubiaceae. Many plants in these families have medicinal, horticultural, spice, or food uses. We also collected edible banana cultivars of the Solomon Islands (reportedly numbering over a hundred) and wild varieties; as global warming continues, bananas may become one of the important sources of dietary starch. In the second five-year phase, we had originally planned to expand the area covered in key regions and to strengthen collecting and surveys of important and endemic species, so as to gain a clearer picture of the vegetation and plant distribution across the entire country and to present the diversity of this biodiversity hotspot. Unfortunately, for various reasons the project could not continue! Nevertheless, more than 10,000 plant specimens and dried materials such as DNA samples are now stored in the NMNS Herbarium (TNM), and live plants are kept in KBCC's net houses and quarantine net houses. We also published two books: Field Guide to the Plants of Solomon Islands (bilingual) and Sol Amazing: Lycophytes & Ferns of the Solomon Islands. Interested readers may request copies from the author at NMNS by covering the postage. Perhaps this, too, is the result of five years of work: Taiwan's contribution to the conservation of the world's biodiversity.
